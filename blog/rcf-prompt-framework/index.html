Prompt Engineering

The R-C-F Framework:
Prompts That Actually Work

4 min read Prompt Engineering May 2025 Works on any AI

Bad prompts get bad outputs. It's not the AI's fault — it's the brief. Most people write prompts like they're Googling: a few keywords and a vague hope. The R-C-F framework is a three-part structure that gives any AI model enough to produce something genuinely useful on the first pass.

Works with

This framework is model-agnostic. It works on Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity — any large language model. The examples below use Claude because it follows structured prompts most reliably, but the structure itself applies universally.

The Three Components

R
Role
Tell the AI who it is. Not "you are an AI assistant" — that's useless. A specific role with domain expertise and a defined perspective.
C
Context
Give it the situation. Who is the audience? What's the goal? What constraints matter? What does it already need to know to answer well?
F
Format
Specify the output. Length, structure, tone, what to include and what to leave out. Don't leave this to chance — the AI will guess wrong.

That's it. Three components. Each one is doing different work — Role sets the model's perspective and expertise, Context gives it the raw materials, Format controls what it does with them. Leave any one out and the output degrades noticeably.

Five Before / After Examples

The same request, written as a weak prompt and then as an R-C-F prompt. The difference in output quality is immediate.

Example 1: Email draft

Before (weak)Weak
Write an email to a client about a delayed project.
After (R-C-F)Strong
Role: You are a senior account manager who is direct, professional, and values the client relationship above all else.

Context: I'm emailing a long-term client (Sarah at Meridian Design) to tell her a website project is delayed by two weeks due to a supplier issue on our end. She's busy and doesn't like excuses. We have a strong relationship and I want to keep it.

Format: Write the email in under 120 words. Lead with the delay and the new date, then the reason (briefly, no blame-shifting), then what we're doing to minimise the impact. Close warmly. No "I hope this email finds you well."

Example 2: Social media caption

Before (weak)Weak
Write an Instagram caption for a new product launch.
After (R-C-F)Strong
Role: You are a UK-based content creator with a no-nonsense voice. You don't use hype or emojis excessively. Your audience trusts you because you're always honest.

Context: Launching a 200-prompt AI pack aimed at freelancers and founders who are serious about saving time. The pack costs £9 and has just gone live. The Instagram audience is 25–40, UK-based, already familiar with AI tools.

Format: One caption, under 100 words. Hook in the first line (no question openers). End with a CTA to click the link in bio. Two relevant hashtags maximum, at the end only.

Example 3: Research summary

Before (weak)Weak
Summarise AI regulation in the UK.
After (R-C-F)Strong
Role: You are a policy analyst who translates complex regulatory frameworks into plain English for non-lawyers.

Context: The audience is a UK-based SME owner who has no legal background. They want to understand what AI regulations they actually need to pay attention to in 2025 — specifically around data handling, bias, and liability. They don't need historical background, just the current and upcoming landscape.

Format: Structured with three subheadings (Data, Bias & Transparency, Liability). Under each, 2–3 sentences maximum. End with one sentence on the most urgent action to take. Total: under 250 words.

Example 4: Meeting notes

Before (weak)Weak
Clean up these meeting notes.
After (R-C-F)Strong
Role: You are an executive assistant who is known for producing crystal-clear meeting summaries that people actually read.

Context: These are rough notes from a 45-minute strategy meeting between three co-founders. The notes include tangents, unresolved debates, and some shorthand only I understand. The summary will be shared with the full team who weren't in the room.

Format: Three sections: (1) Key decisions made, (2) Actions with owner and deadline, (3) Open questions still unresolved. Bullet points only. Remove any content that was explicitly marked "parking lot." Do not add commentary or summarise the discussion — just extract the outputs.

Example 5: Landing page copy

Before (weak)Weak
Write copy for my AI tool landing page.
After (R-C-F)Strong
Role: You are a direct-response copywriter in the style of David Ogilvy — evidence-led, benefit-first, zero filler.

Context: The product is a £9 digital prompt pack. The buyer is a time-poor freelancer or founder who is sceptical of AI hype. They've seen bad AI content and want to believe good AI is possible. The biggest objection is "this won't be any different from what I already try." The biggest desire is getting more done in less time without sacrificing quality.

Format: Hero section only. Headline (max 8 words), subheadline (max 20 words), three bullet benefits (max 10 words each), CTA button text (max 5 words). No emojis.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Vague roles: "Act as an expert" tells the model nothing. "Act as a UK-based chartered accountant specialising in SME tax" tells it everything.
  • Missing audience: Without knowing who the output is for, the AI defaults to a generic middle. Always specify who will read it.
  • No length guidance: If you don't specify length, you'll get whatever the model thinks is appropriate — usually too long.
  • Skipping the negative: Adding what to exclude is often as important as what to include. "No bullet points" or "don't use the word synergy" sharpens the output significantly.
